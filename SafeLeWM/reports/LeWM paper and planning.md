# LeWM paper and planning

**LeWorldModel provides a compact predictive model that can support visual control, but its strongest headline results concern nearby goals.** Its contribution is a simple way to learn both visual representations and action-conditioned dynamics without a pretrained image encoder or pixel reconstruction. The resulting model can evaluate candidate actions before the simulator executes them. Anchored Planning, or AP, is a separate extension that uses recorded experience to choose intermediate targets for that frozen model. This distinction matters for our pipeline: learning a world model, searching for actions, retrieving targets and learning a safety adapter are four different computations, with different evidence and costs.

The original paper is Lucas Maes and colleagues' *LeWorldModel: Stable End-to-End Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture from Pixels*. This review uses **arXiv v3, dated 3 June 2026**, saved as [the local paper](../../docs/papers/LeWorldModel-2603.19312v3.pdf). The repository-linked [v1](../../docs/papers/LeWorldModel-2603.19312v1.pdf) is also preserved. Adjacent provenance files record source URLs and SHA256 hashes. AP's [companion paper](../../docs/papers/AnchoredPlanning-2609.30036v4.pdf), *Aim Short to Reach Far*, is saved separately. These are distinct papers and protocols. [LeWM version history](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19312); [AP v4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30036v4).

**What the model learns.** Imagine a PushT observation showing the pusher and block. An encoder converts the image into a vector, `z_t = E(o_t)`. A predictor takes that representation and a proposed action, producing a prediction of the next representation, `predicted_z_(t+1) = F(z_t, a_t)`. The actual future image passes through the same encoder, giving a target against which to compare the prediction. The model therefore learns which visual changes follow which actions. Its training data contain image/action sequences rather than reward labels; expert or heuristic behaviour still determines which situations those sequences cover. The compact notation hides a causal history window in the actual predictor. [LeWM, §3.1, pp. 4-5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.19312v3#page=4).

The default architecture combines an approximately five-million-parameter ViT-Tiny encoder with a ten-million-parameter transformer predictor. Actions condition the predictor through Adaptive LayerNorm. The model predicts in its learned representation space, so planning does not require generating future images. The decoded images shown in the paper come from a separate diagnostic decoder. Separate pretrained checkpoints are released for TwoRoom, Reacher, PushT and Cube; this is not a demonstrated universal checkpoint for all four environments. [LeWM, §3.1 and Appendix D](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.19312v3); [official release](https://github.com/lucas-maes/le-wm).

**The training problem is keeping the representation informative.** Prediction error alone has a useless perfect solution: encode every image as the same vector and always predict that vector. LeWM adds SIGReg, which encourages the distribution of encoded observations to resemble an isotropic Gaussian. It tests projections of a batch of embeddings onto random directions, using a one-dimensional distribution test. Intuitively, prediction rewards forecasting accuracy while regularization prevents all observations from becoming indistinguishable. The encoder and predictor train jointly, with gradients through both image branches and no target-network moving average or stop-gradient mechanism. [LeWM, equations 1-3, p. 5; Appendix A](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.19312v3#page=5).

This is the useful interpretation of the paper's claim about one effective loss hyperparameter: one coefficient balances prediction and regularization. It does not mean the whole system needs only one setting. The appendix shows substantial sensitivity to predictor dropout, predictor size and the action optimizer. For example, the reported PushT mean changes from 78% with no predictor dropout to 96% at dropout 0.1. Also, a Gaussian distribution of representations is not a probability distribution over future hazards. It supplies neither collision probabilities nor calibrated uncertainty. The latter is an implication of what the objective measures, not an additional result claimed by the paper. [LeWM, Table 9, p. 26](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.19312v3#page=26).

**How a frozen model becomes a controller.** At deployment, encode the current observation and a goal image. Generate candidate action sequences, predict their outcomes and score the final predicted representation by its squared distance from the goal representation. Cross-Entropy Method, or CEM, repeatedly samples candidates, keeps the best and refits its sampling distribution. Here CEM optimizes actions for the current decision; it does not update the world-model weights or learn our adapter. The simulator then executes selected actions and supplies another observation. [LeWM, §3.2 and Figure 4, pp. 4-6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.19312v3#page=4).

The units matter. The original recipe groups five environment actions into each model action block, plans five blocks, and says it executes the resulting **25-action sequence before replanning**. It uses 300 candidates and 30 elites, with up to 30 CEM iterations for PushT and ten for the other environments. Our AP reproduction instead replans after five actions. A configuration field such as `horizon: 5` is therefore insufficient without its action-block and execution semantics. Longer prediction also compounds model error, so increasing the horizon is not an automatic solution to difficult goals. [LeWM, Appendix D, pp. 17-18](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.19312v3#page=17); [our AP protocol](../PushTAPReproduction.md).

**What the headline success rates actually measure.** The main evaluation samples a starting point from an offline trajectory and chooses a goal **25 timesteps later in that trajectory**, with an allowance of 50 executed actions. Figure 6 reports these rounded success percentages:

| Environment | LeWM | PLDM | DINO-WM, pixels only |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| TwoRoom | 87% | 97% | 100% |
| Reacher | 86% | 78% | 79% |
| PushT | 96% | 78% | 74% |
| OGBench Cube | 74% | 65% | 86% |

These are the paper's reported rates, not reconstructed episode counts. The protocol supports useful short-goal control, particularly on PushT and Reacher, while showing that LeWM does not win every task. It does not establish 96% completion of arbitrary full PushT episodes. Table 5 evaluates three model-training seeds on the same 50 PushT trajectories, reporting 96.0 ± 2.83 for LeWM. Its caption calls the dispersion “variance”, so that number should not be relabelled as a confidence interval. [LeWM, Figure 6, p. 7; Appendix F.1, p. 19; Table 5, p. 24](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.19312v3).

The efficiency evidence is relevant to repeated planning. Figure 3 reports 0.98 seconds versus 47 seconds for its plotted planning computation, approximately a 48-fold ratio against DINO-WM. That is a particular planning measurement, not an end-to-end episode runtime or a promise of real-time control on our rental. The abstract reports roughly 15 million parameters and training in a few hours on one GPU. V1 explicitly names an L40S, but v3 removes that hardware paragraph. We can avoid world-model pretraining by loading released weights; online candidate scoring remains a significant cost. [LeWM, Figure 3, p. 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.19312v3#page=3); [v1, Appendix D](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.19312v1#page=20).

**What AP changes.** A short-horizon planner can favour moving directly toward the final goal even when the task requires first moving away from it. AP changes the target. It retrieves an experience segment whose beginning resembles the current observation and whose distant endpoint resembles the goal, then takes an observed image five actions after that segment's beginning as an intermediate target. LeWM still predicts from the live observation. AP-CEM searches for actions toward the retrieved target using observation-only deployment memory; AP-rank additionally uses recorded action blocks. [AP, §3 and Figure 2, pp. 4-5](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.30036v4#S3).

An important input is the supplied recorded start-to-goal offset `H`. Retrieval uses a shrinking span `h = max(5, H - t)`, where `t` is the number of executed actions. This is not a learned estimate of how long an arbitrary goal will take. On AP's longer PushT protocol, with offset 140 and allowance 280 actions, AP-CEM obtains 89/128 completions. Our separately audited reproduction obtained 88/128 with the authors' evaluator and 89/128 with our wrapper. These results support baseline competence under matched inputs; they do not alter the original LeWM short-goal results. AP's appendix also describes a 24-action goal offset in its account of released LeWM evaluation, whereas LeWM's paper specifies 25. Exact indexing belongs in the software parity checks. [AP, Table 1 and Appendices A.1, D.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.30036v4); [our reproduction](../results/15-pusht-ap-reproduction.md).

**What we inherit for safety research.** LeWM's planning objective measures goal proximity. It does not penalize our forbidden regions or enforce a requested task sequence. Its surprise experiments detect prediction errors after artificial object teleportation; a predictable collision could produce little surprise. Representation quality also varies by quantity: the Cube appendix reports strong position decoding but weaker orientation decoding. These findings motivate separate checks of safety information, prediction accuracy, planning reachability and adapter authority. They do not establish that any chosen adapter can repair all four. [LeWM, Figure 8 and §6, p. 9; Table 4, p. 21](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.19312v3).

For our pipeline, the practical implication is to preserve the full controller specification: task-specific checkpoint, image transforms, action normalization, renderer, history length, action-block units, retrieval membership, cache precision and query construction. Track model queries separately from simulator actions. The earlier hardware suggestion remains a provisioning estimate until measured with our exact configuration. Our validated PushT screen already showed why task competence and safe adapter authority require separate evidence: native completed 12/16 cases but violated in every case, and the tested adapter heads failed useful authority. That completed screen remains closed. [AP runtime guide](https://github.com/daybraeklaxry/Aim-Short-to-Reach-Far/blob/main/code/ap/README.md); [our qualification result](../results/16-pusht-validated.md).

A productive reading order is Figure 1 and §3.1 for learning, Figure 4 and §3.2 for control, then Appendix D and F.1 for the actual units and evaluation protocol. Read AP Figure 2 afterward. This order makes the research question precise: whether an additional learned component can recover safe task completion around an already useful frozen predictive model and controller, with its information, authority and computational budget explicitly measured.
