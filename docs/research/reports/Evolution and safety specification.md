# Evolution and safety specification

**Evolution can reduce the manual design of safe behaviour and some safety-performance tuning. It still needs information about what counts as unacceptable, and selection alone does not enforce safety during execution.** The promising research question is therefore which human decisions evolutionary learning can replace, while preserving useful task completion and a defensible safety claim. Existing work supplies several mechanisms: separate feasibility from reward, retain multiple performance-risk tradeoffs, learn constraints from demonstrations, or evolve controllers that are subsequently verified. These mechanisms remove different burdens and retain different assumptions. [Deb, 2000](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0045782599003898); [NSGA-II, 2002](https://web.njit.edu/~horacio/Math451H/download/2002-6-2-DEB-NSGA-II.pdf); [CoCoRL, 2024](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v238/lindner24a.html); [Verdier and Mazo, 2018](https://arxiv.org/html/1812.02711v1).

For our reasoning, it helps to separate five design choices. The safety definition identifies what deserves protection. A detector or model recognises relevant events. A behavioural strategy determines how the robot avoids them. A selection rule specifies how safety and task performance affect learning. Optimizer settings determine how the search proceeds. Eliminating a reward penalty can leave the other four choices intact. Learning a detector moves some work into data and assumptions. Learning a recovery strategy can remove detailed behavioural rules while retaining an explicit outcome requirement. A binary violation flag is still a safety specification and can be represented mathematically as a cost.

Consider a **hypothetical fragile-cup task**. A person supplies the goal, moving the cup to a shelf, and an outcome signal that recognises breakage or spilling. Evolution searches over candidate grasping and movement strategies. It could discover gentler acceleration or a different grasp without a person prescribing a force profile or a detailed sequence of corrective actions. This possibility depends on the observations and controller representation allowing those strategies. The human still supplies the meaning of success and failure, the evaluation conditions and the rule for accepting a candidate. A policy that never touches the cup also needs to remain distinguishable from safe completion.

**Feasibility-first selection can remove a reward-penalty coefficient.** Deb's method prefers a feasible candidate over an infeasible one, compares task performance between feasible candidates, and compares violation among infeasible candidates. Reward and violation therefore do not have to be combined into one weighted number. In our illustrative task, policies meeting a declared safety criterion could compete on completion. The original work concerns numerical constrained optimization; it establishes a selection mechanism rather than a manipulation safety result. It also retains supplied constraints and a measure of violation. [Deb, 2000](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0045782599003898).

Multiobjective evolution offers a related option. NSGA-II maintains nondominated alternatives, allowing observed completion and risk to remain separate objectives. A population can preserve several tradeoffs without choosing one reward-versus-risk weight in advance. Selecting a deployment policy still requires an acceptability decision. A fast risky policy can be Pareto-optimal, and different policies can remain incomparable. Population size, variation operators and other search choices also remain. This removes one kind of tuning while making the unresolved tradeoff visible. [NSGA-II, 2002](https://web.njit.edu/~horacio/Math451H/download/2002-6-2-DEB-NSGA-II.pdf).

These formulations are not exclusive to evolution. Constrained Policy Optimization also separates task reward from constraints. The scientific question concerns the benefit of evolutionary search under a particular information structure and budget; separating safety from reward does not itself establish that benefit. [Achiam et al., 2017](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v70/achiam17a.html).

**Learning constraints can reduce explicit specification work.** CoCoRL learns shared constraints from safe demonstrations even when the demonstrations' rewards are unknown and differ. Its feasibility guarantee depends on assumptions including safe demonstrators and an appropriate feature representation. Its notion of safety is feasibility in a constrained Markov decision process, involving expected cumulative costs. Its evaluations are gridworlds and driving simulation. An evolutionary search could use such inferred constraints, but that integration is our inference rather than an evolutionary result established by the paper. [Lindner et al., 2024](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v238/lindner24a.html).

Demonstrations do not uniquely identify every safety boundary. Several constraints can explain the same observed behaviour, an ambiguity addressed explicitly by uncertainty-aware constraint-learning research. For the cup example, successful demonstrations can communicate acceptable handling, but their silence about an unseen hazard is not evidence that the hazard is acceptable. Learning moves part of the specification burden into examples, representations and inference assumptions. [Chou et al., 2021](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v155/chou21a/chou21a.pdf).

**Evolution can synthesize a safety mechanism whose properties are checked separately.** Verdier and Mazo use genetic programming to construct controllers and control Lyapunov-barrier functions, followed by SMT verification. Their system receives the dynamics and safe, initial and goal sets. Evolution proposes candidates; verification checks the required properties; the controller governs execution. The reported examples involve dynamical systems up to third order, and some auxiliary functions remained hand-synthesized. This is a direct precedent for evolutionary safety synthesis, with a substantially narrower scope than contact-rich manipulation. Search is also not guaranteed to find a solution within a finite number of generations. [Verdier and Mazo, CDC 2018](https://arxiv.org/html/1812.02711v1).

This distinction gives “enforce” a precise role. An evolved mechanism can help enforce a specified property when its implementation and assumptions support that claim. A collection of successful rollouts supplies empirical evidence instead. Neither establishes correctness for an inaccurate world model or unmodelled hazard automatically.

**SafeAPT provides a concrete manipulation precedent with explicit remaining design choices.** It evolves a MAP-Elites policy repertoire in simulation, then uses constrained Bayesian optimization and an online safety model to select behaviours for a physical robot. Its Kuka hockey task defines safety through a minimum puck distance from the table edge. The paper supplies safety scores, a safety limit and modelling choices, and tunes some hyperparameters through simulation grid search. Hardware experiments reported no violations in eight repeats, each budgeted for 15 episodes; some simulated conditions had violations. Its probabilistic argument depends on the safety model's accuracy. This supports evolutionary contribution within a hybrid safety-aware system, with outcome definitions and tuning still present. [SafeAPT, 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2201.13248).

For any empirical evolutionary approach, feasibility estimated from finite trials remains uncertain. No observed breakage does not establish zero breakage probability, and repeatedly selecting on the same scenarios can favour policies that exploit that evaluation set. Selection also occurs after candidates have been evaluated, so a safe final policy does not make all search-time evaluations safe. The distinction between safety during learning and safety at deployment is explicit in safe-learning formulations. [Wachi et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.02025v2).

The resulting research question is: **Can evolutionary learning discover useful safe manipulation behaviour from explicit outcome-level safety information, while reducing manually designed behavioural rules and task-specific penalty tuning?** Evidence for that claim would need to account for the work retained in monitors, representations, thresholds, demonstrations and search configuration. Otherwise manual design could simply move to a different part of the system. Evolutionary superiority is a separate question. The immediate intellectual task is to identify the design burden worth reducing and the kind of safety claim that must survive its removal.
