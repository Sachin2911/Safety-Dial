# Safety in robotic manipulation

**Robot-learning research does not use one operational definition of safety.** It uses several ways of specifying undesirable outcomes and several different standards for claiming that an agent avoids them. For our research, a useful working interpretation is: a manipulation system acts within stated limits on undesirable consequences, under specified environmental and uncertainty assumptions. This is a synthesis, not a universal definition. Useful safe manipulation additionally requires achieving the intended task. The central distinctions are what deserves protection, which events or behaviours threaten it, and what evidence supports the safety claim. The control and safe-RL literature makes these choices explicit through state constraints, cost budgets, probability bounds and other formulations. [Brunke et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2108.06266v2); [Wachi et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.02025v2).

This selective reading map combines established foundations with recent manipulation work available on 3 November 2026. It is intended to help define the research problem before choosing an architecture or optimizer.

**First distinguish what is protected from how it is protected.** Human physical safety concerns injury, while robot safety concerns the integrity and admissible operation of the robot. A foundational human-robot interaction study evaluates injury potential through crash-testing, illustrating the difference between detecting contact and measuring consequences for a person. Recent operational-space control work instead expresses requirements through obstacle avoidance, self-collision avoidance, joint limits, workspace containment and other robot constraints. These are overlapping concerns, but their measurements are different. [Haddadin et al., 2007](https://roboticsproceedings.org/rss03/p28.pdf); [Morton and Pavone, 2025](https://stanfordasl.github.io/oscbf/).

Object and environment protection introduce another perspective. Manipulation usually requires contact, so avoiding all contact cannot be its general safety objective. In door opening, SafeDiff distinguishes useful opening forces from orthogonal forces that can damage the mechanism or robot. Its chosen damage threshold is an experimental assumption, not a universal boundary between safe and unsafe interaction. This illustrates how a physical concern becomes an operational measurement. [Wei et al., SafeDiff](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.10349v2).

Context can matter even when geometry and forces appear acceptable. Carrying water above electronics presents a concern that collision avoidance alone does not express. *Semantically Safe Robot Manipulation* examines this kind of relationship between object meaning, scene context and motion constraints. Safety can also depend on history: a contaminated object touching a clean one, or releasing an object before it is contained. The 2026 *SafeManip* preprint evaluates such temporal requirements separately from task completion. [Brunke et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.15185); [Huang et al., SafeManip](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.12386v3).

**The same hazard can support different mathematical safety questions.** Consider our own illustrative example of transferring a fragile cup. A per-step requirement could limit grip force at every instant. An expected cumulative-cost requirement could bound average accumulated force-limit exceedance across transfers. A whole-episode chance constraint could bound the probability that any drop or break occurs during a transfer. These formulations answer different questions; a bound on average accumulated violations is not automatically a bound on the probability of any bad episode. Wachi and colleagues explicitly distinguish instantaneous, cumulative, expectation-based and probability-based formulations. [Wachi et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.02025v2).

Constrained Policy Optimization provides a representative safe-RL formulation: maximize task return while bounding expected auxiliary costs. It is therefore essential to inspect what the cost counts and over which horizon it accumulates. In the special case where an undiscounted cost records only the first failure, expected total cost can equal failure probability. That equivalence comes from the cost construction, not from the phrase “constrained RL.” [Achiam et al., 2017](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v70/achiam17a.html).

Frequency and severity also differ. A rare broken cup and frequent harmless brushes can produce different rankings depending on the cost definition. Tail-risk methods such as conditional value at risk focus on the costly end of the outcome distribution rather than its mean. They still require a meaningful account of severity. [Chow et al., 2015](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/6014-risk-sensitive-and-robust-decision-making-a-cvar-optimization-approach.pdf).

Control barrier functions take another perspective: safety means that trajectories starting inside a specified safe set remain inside it. The claim depends on a model, available controls and the conditions under which the barrier constraint can be maintained. Reachability methods ask which states admit actions that avoid failure despite specified disturbances. For our cup illustration, “not broken yet” differs from “still in a state from which the robot can prevent breakage.” [Ames et al., 2019](https://coogan.ece.gatech.edu/papers/pdf/amesecc19.pdf); [Fisac et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/1705.01292v3).

A temporal requirement adds another dimension: do not release the cup until it is supported. Monitoring this requirement needs information about the execution sequence, not just its final state. SafeManip uses finite-trace temporal specifications for this purpose. Its claims are limited to specified properties grounded in simulator predicates; it does not establish the absence of every possible hazard. [Huang et al., 2026 preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.12386v3).

**When safety applies is as important as how it is measured.** Learning a safe final policy and maintaining safety throughout learning are separate objectives. This distinction appears explicitly in safe-learning formulations. [Wachi et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.02025v2). Applied to evolution, our inference is straightforward: a safe selected individual does not make all the candidate evaluations used to discover it safe. Simulation changes the physical consequences of those evaluations, but it leaves the question of transfer to the real system.

Similarly, observed violation rates, runtime monitoring and mathematical guarantees supply different evidence. A monitor checks a realised execution. An empirical evaluation supports a claim about the tested conditions. A controller guarantee applies under its stated assumptions. Neither a good benchmark score nor a theorem establishes unrestricted real-world harmlessness. Brunke and colleagues distinguish methods that encourage safety from methods intended to certify it. [Brunke et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2108.06266v2).

For our project, this suggests keeping task completion and safety separate before considering their combination. A robot can finish after causing damage; it can also avoid some violations by never attempting the task. Robustness is another separate question: which of these outcomes persists when conditions change? These are conceptual distinctions for our own analysis, rather than a claim that one metric settles all three.

**Start the reading with one overview and one concrete example.** Read Brunke et al., *Safe Learning in Robotics* (2022), beginning with Section 2 and its hard, probabilistic and soft constraints. It provides the vocabulary connecting control and RL. Then read the introduction, Section 3.1 and Section 4.2 of *SafeManip* (2026 preprint) to see how temporal safety becomes an evaluable manipulation specification. Read the property definitions before concentrating on model rankings. [Brunke paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2108.06266v2); [SafeManip paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.12386v3).

The next two readings deepen the formulations before choosing algorithms. Read Wachi, Shen and Sui’s *A Survey of Constraint Formulations in Safe Reinforcement Learning* (IJCAI 2024), focusing on how expectation, probability and time horizon change the problem. Then read Sections I and II of *Control Barrier Functions: Theory and Applications* (2019) for safe-set invariance. The survey benefits from RL and probability background, while the barrier-function paper requires more differential equations and control theory. [Wachi survey](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.02025v2); [CBF paper](https://arxiv.org/html/1903.11199v1).

For watching, begin with Angela Schoellig’s 2020 talk, *Machine Learning for Robotics: Achieving Safety, Performance and Reliability by Combining Models and Data in a Closed-Loop System Architecture*, linked from her lab’s safe-learning page. Then use Aaron Ames’s *Safety-Critical Control of Autonomous Systems* (IPAM, 2020) alongside the barrier-function reading. These are foundational talks, rather than updates on 2026 benchmarks. The official descriptions and recording links were verified; the IPAM player itself could not be tested through the research tool. [Schoellig recording](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tMB5tU8buQ4); [official lab listing](https://www.dynsyslab.org/safe-robot-learning/); [Ames talk page](https://www.mathinstitutes.org/videos/16688).

For each paper, write down the protected entity, the prohibited event, its measurable proxy, the risk measure and horizon, and the assumptions behind the evidence. Then ask what the human supplied and what the learning process discovered. That last question connects this literature directly to our ambition: evolution can search for behaviour under safety requirements, but changing the optimizer does not by itself eliminate the need to specify what should be protected.
