# Safe robotic manipulation research

**Safe robotic manipulation research already offers several concrete ways to constrain actions, learn recovery behaviour, regulate contact, interpret contextual hazards and evaluate execution histories.** These contributions act at different parts of a robot system. A controller can restrict joint motion, a learned critic can trigger recovery, a world model can anticipate a failure, and a benchmark can label a violation after it occurs. Their results therefore answer different questions. The following selective primary-source map covers established foundations and work available by 3 November 2026. It focuses on explicitly safety-oriented methods and their evidence, rather than treating high task success as proof of safety. [OSCBF](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.06736v3); [Recovery RL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.15920); [UNISafe](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.00779v2); [SafeManip](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.12386v3).

**Physical safety controllers restrict what reaches the actuators.** Morton and Pavone's *Safe, Task-Consistent Manipulation with Operational Space Control Barrier Functions*, presented at IROS 2025, filters nominal commands while respecting a task hierarchy. OSCBF handles whole-body collision avoidance, workspace and joint limits, and singularities. Simulation and Franka hardware experiments evaluate constraint satisfaction and tracking. The method requires dynamics, geometry, barrier definitions, limits and controller settings. Its guarantees depend on maintaining feasible constraints: conflicts can make the optimisation infeasible, and relaxation can remove the strict guarantee. This establishes a mechanism for specified physical restrictions, without claiming to detect every contextual hazard. [OSCBF paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.06736v3).

Energy-based control addresses another aspect of interaction. Michel, Saveriano and Lee's *A Novel Safety-Aware Energy Tank Formulation Based on Control Barrier Functions*, published in RA-L in 2024, combines energy accounting with energy and power constraints. Hardware tests include force control, stiffness changes and kinetic-energy limitation. Tank bounds, power limits and control settings remain supplied. Its time-varying kinetic-energy example reports occasional power-bound violations because the formulation does not handle time-varying barriers. This is a useful reminder to inspect the implemented extension as well as the underlying theorem. [Energy-tank paper](https://www.inverse-project.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Michel-et-al.-A-Novel-Safety-Aware-Energy-Tank.pdf).

Passivity itself concerns how a system exchanges energy. It does not automatically establish acceptable force, velocity or injury risk. Shaw Cortez and colleagues explicitly address that distinction by combining passive interaction with operating-region and velocity constraints in their ICRA 2021 work. [Safe, Passive Control for Mechanical Systems](https://people.kth.se/~dimos/pdfs/ZCBF_Mechanical_Systems__conf_Final.pdf). For our interpretation, physical protection therefore needs to be stated at the level actually controlled: geometry, force, velocity, power or another specified property.

**Recovery learning separates task pursuit from corrective behaviour.** Thananjeyan and colleagues' *Recovery RL: Safe Reinforcement Learning with Learned Recovery Zones*, published in RA-L/ICRA in 2021, uses offline constraint data to learn a risk critic and a recovery policy before task learning. The task policy pursues reward while recovery intervenes near predicted violations. Its evaluation includes simulated contact-rich manipulation and physical obstacle avoidance. The physical experiment should not be described as hardware validation of all its manipulation tasks. Constraint labels and the switching criterion remain provided. [Recovery RL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.15920).

Zhang and colleagues extend this idea to contact compliance in *SRL-VIC: A Variable Stiffness-Based Safe Reinforcement Learning for Contact-Rich Robotic Tasks*, RA-L 2024. The policy chooses displacement and stiffness, with a learned safety critic controlling task/recovery switching. A force threshold defines safety, while scripted contacts supply offline labels and reward penalties remain designed. MuJoCo training transfers to a Franka arm navigating a narrow contact-rich maze. The paper reports violations during learning and a changed maze where behaviour avoids violations but fails to find the exit. Its contribution combines learned movement, compliance and recovery; its results also expose the distinction between avoiding failure and completing safely. [SRL-VIC](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.13744v1).

A recent extension, Shithil and Liu's *SEVI: Safe Evidential Real-World Reinforcement Learning*, uses estimated risk and epistemic uncertainty to gate recovery during online learning. The October 2026 manuscript reports RA-L acceptance and includes hardware USB insertion. Annotated failures, recovery demonstrations and calibrated task-specific thresholds remain necessary. The evidence concerns reduced unsafe events, rather than their universal elimination. [SEVI manuscript](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.31829v1).

**Predictive safety filters evaluate consequences before allowing a policy to act.** Seo, Nakamura and Bajcsy's *Uncertainty-aware Latent Safety Filters for Avoiding Out-of-Distribution Failures*, or UNISafe, appeared at CoRL 2025. It combines latent reachability, a learned fallback policy and uncertainty estimates, using conformal calibration to detect uncertain imagined trajectories. Its manipulation studies include simulated block plucking and physical Franka Jenga. Offline failure labels define the learned failure margin, and intervention settings remain specified. [UNISafe](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.00779v2).

The method addresses a particular weakness of model-based filtering: a predictor can be confident about familiar transitions while becoming unreliable outside its training experience. UNISafe also checks whether the fallback remains unreliable, allowing the system to stop and request human help. Its appendix explicitly limits the assurance because learned dynamics and reinforcement learning can fail. The conformal result concerns a detection component; it is not a calibrated probability of every real-world manipulation failure. [UNISafe, Appendix B.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.00779v2). Our inference is that the filter, its failure labels, its fallback authority and its uncertainty handling must all be assessed together.

**Force-aware learned manipulation modifies contact using feedback.** Wei and colleagues' *Ensuring Force Safety in Vision-Guided Robotic Manipulation via Implicit Tactile Calibration*, or SafeDiff, appeared at CoRL 2025. It generates future robot states, uses force feedback to calibrate the sequence, and derives actions from the corrected plan. Door-opening experiments distinguish forces that help opening from off-trajectory forces that can damage the interaction. The paper supplies a dataset and simulation and hardware evaluations. [SafeDiff proceedings](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v305/wei25a.html).

Its damage criterion assumes a 20 N threshold, rather than establishing a universal physical damage boundary. Evaluation also measures force averages, maxima and time below thresholds. The resulting evidence supports force reduction on the studied tasks; it does not provide a general formal bound for every object or contact. SafeDiff and SRL-VIC intervene at different places: one calibrates a planned state sequence using sensed force, while the other learns displacement and stiffness with a recovery gate. [SafeDiff paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.10349v2); [SRL-VIC](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.13744v1).

**Semantic safety connects the meaning of a scene to restrictions on behaviour.** Brunke and colleagues' *Semantically Safe Robot Manipulation: From Semantic Scene Understanding to Motion Safeguards*, published in RA-L in 2025, builds a semantic scene representation and uses contextual reasoning to identify unsafe relationships. These become control-barrier constraints alongside geometric restrictions. Examples include carrying water above electronics and rotating a filled cup. Hardware demonstrations use both teleoperation and learned policies. This is an actual runtime filter, with supplied constraint types and barrier formulations. Correct enforcement of the resulting constraint does not itself certify that perception and language reasoning identified every hazard. [Semantically Safe Robot Manipulation](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.15185).

Policy alignment offers a different intervention. Zhang and colleagues' *SafeVLA: Towards Safety Alignment of Vision-Language-Action Model via Constrained Learning*, NeurIPS 2025, fine-tunes policies with supplied safety costs and adaptive Lagrange multipliers. Its tasks include navigation, pickup and fetching, with hazards such as collisions and collateral object disturbance. Most evidence is simulated; a narrower physical pickup-transfer demonstration requires additional perception and control engineering. Safety rules, cost budgets and optimisation choices remain supplied. Adaptive multipliers reduce dependence on a fixed tradeoff coefficient, but the reported safety claim remains empirical. [SafeVLA](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.03480v3).

Names can obscure where protection operates. Tayal and Nambi's September 2026 *ShieldVLA* preprint uses a vision-language rubric and learned critic for feasibility-aware fine-tuning, then deploys a single policy **without a runtime shield**. Its experiments are simulated, and the paper explicitly identifies its safety evidence as empirical. This is relevant policy-learning work, with a different execution architecture from a filter that can override commands. [ShieldVLA](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.13231v1).

**Specification-driven benchmarks make failures visible without preventing them.** Huang and colleagues' 2026 *SafeManip* preprint evaluates temporal properties concerning contact, release stability, contamination, containment and other execution-history requirements. Authored specifications operate over simulator-grounded predicate traces. The framework distinguishes properties that were actually tested from those whose triggering conditions never occurred. Its limitations identify privileged simulator state as a barrier to direct hardware use, where predicates would need noisy sensor estimates. The monitor is an evaluation mechanism. [SafeManip](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.12386v3).

Peng and colleagues' 2026 *ManiGuard* preprint combines a benchmark, safety-annotated demonstrations and fine-tuning experiments. It separates task completion, safety and engagement. Authored temporal specifications provide simulator labels, and the monitor checks execution **without terminating it**. The paper's physical Franka evaluation uses operator-scored safety verdicts, rather than the simulator-state automaton. Fine-tuning can change behaviour, but the benchmark monitor itself never supplies protective actions. [ManiGuard](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.17386v1).

For our research, these studies suggest comparing three things in each system: the source of its safety information, the mechanism that changes behaviour, and the evidence supporting its claim. A learned risk critic, a force threshold, a semantic rubric and a temporal specification impose different engineering burdens. A reduction in one burden should be assessed alongside the others retained. Cross-paper results also concern different tasks and hazards, so they cannot establish an optimizer or architecture ranking without a matched comparison.

For a focused reading route, start with **OSCBF**, **SRL-VIC**, **UNISafe**, **Semantically Safe Robot Manipulation** and **SafeVLA**. They contrast explicit physical constraints, learned recovery and compliance, predictive intervention, contextual constraint generation and policy alignment. Keep SafeManip and ManiGuard as references for what the resulting behaviour should be measured against. This map establishes concrete neighbouring approaches for our work; it does not determine our architecture, authorise an experiment or establish a novelty claim.
