# Study adapters before choosing their interface

For our project, start with **control adapters: components that correct actions, shape planning, or condition an existing policy**. That is the literature closest to the TwoRoom residual and Block Plucking selector. Internal neural adapters such as LoRA are useful background, but they answer a different question about how to restrict parameter updates. The learning sequence below focuses on six topics, with papers, accessible explanations and YouTube companions. The aim is to understand where a shared adapter could act before choosing its architecture. These resources motivate options; they do not establish that one interface will work across our environments.

Links and available metadata were checked on 4 November 2026 against papers, official project pages, course pages and repositories. Videos were not watched end to end. Some YouTube pages could not be retrieved directly, so their links were verified through the official pages that embed them. Only the two MPC runtimes below were verified.

**Begin with the planner: Understanding Model Predictive Control.** Watch MATLAB's [Why Use Model Predictive Control?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8U0xiOkDcmw), **4:50**, followed by [What Is Model Predictive Control?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cEWnixjNdzs), **6:06**. The official [series page](https://www.mathworks.com/videos/series/understanding-model-predictive-control.html) supplies the videos and runtimes. These introductory explanations cover predicting outcomes, optimizing an action sequence, executing an initial action and replanning. They are the quickest preparation for locating an adapter inside that loop. Our proposed candidate-ranking adapter would intervene between evaluating alternatives and selecting what to execute. That connection is our design interpretation: these videos teach the planning foundation, not an already validated learned adapter for our tasks.

**Then study action correction: Residual Reinforcement Learning for Robot Control.** Start with the authors' [robot demonstration](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RRllUka-r2A), then read the [project page](https://residualrl.github.io/) and [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.03201). Johannink and colleagues combine conventional feedback control with a learned residual and demonstrate real block assembly involving contacts and unstable objects. The central idea is easy to follow: the base proposes a command, and the learned component adds a correction. The RL details are intermediate. This is directly relevant to understanding our TwoRoom interface and interpreting the unsuccessful Block Plucking residual screen. It demonstrates that residual control can work in manipulation, but does not establish the right bounds, inputs or learning method for our system. The video is a demonstration rather than a full lecture.

**Connect compact controllers to evolution: World Models.** Watch [Recurrent World Models Facilitate Policy Evolution](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HzA8LRqhujk) and read Ha and Schmidhuber's [interactive author article](https://worldmodels.github.io/). Their learned visual and recurrent representations feed a deliberately small controller optimized with CMA-ES. This makes the reasoning behind “large learned model, small searchable controller” concrete. Focus on the separation of representation learning from controller training and on the discussion of exploiting mistakes in a learned simulation. It is approachable with basic neural-network knowledge and particularly relevant to our evolutionary motivation. However, their controller directly chooses actions; it is not our neutral correction around an existing planner, and task reward or survival is not our safe-completion criterion. The [author code](https://github.com/hardmaru/WorldModelsExperiments) is useful to inspect, but documents legacy dependencies.

**Next examine adapters that help planning: POPLIN and LOOP.** Read [POPLIN: Exploring Model-based Planning with Policy Networks](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.08649) alongside its [author implementation](https://github.com/WilsonWangTHU/POPLIN). POPLIN distinguishes policy-generated initial action sequences from planning through policy-parameter perturbations. It raises an important possibility: the learned component can improve what the planner searches over instead of editing the executed action. Follow it with the accessible [CMU explanation of LOOP](https://blog.ml.cmu.edu/2022/01/07/loop/) and its [author-linked video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Oj-EMWZB8Mg). LOOP combines learned dynamics, value estimates and actor-guided planning, including a constrained planning variant. These are intermediate readings after MPC. They clarify proposals, scoring and constraints as separate design choices. Neither validates our proposed common reranker or keeps every learned component frozen in the way our study requires.

**For generalisation, study conditioning: Rapid Motor Adaptation.** Watch [RMA: Rapid Motor Adaptation for Legged Robots, Method](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qKF6dr_S-wQ), then inspect the architecture on the [authors' project page](https://ashish-kmr.github.io/rma-legged-robots/) and in the [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.04034). A separate adaptation module estimates an environmental context from recent states and actions, and the base policy uses that estimate. This illustrates an alternative to correcting actions: the adapter supplies information that changes how the controller behaves. Read it for what history contributes and how the interface between modules is prepared. The base policy was explicitly trained to accept that context, so RMA is not a universal module attached to an arbitrary frozen planner. Its robustness across locomotion conditions does not demonstrate identical-weight transfer from navigation to manipulation.

**Finish with the safety distinction: predictive safety filters.** Watch Lukas Hewing's [Model Predictive Control and Predictive Safety Filters](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Zx8QKSUqIW0), listed on the [UC San Diego course page](https://sylvia-herbert-fs9e.squarespace.com/teaching). Pair it with Wabersich and Zeilinger's [predictive safety filter paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05506). This is the most mathematical core resource. A filter checks whether a proposed command can satisfy future constraints and modifies it when necessary, using a dynamics model and uncertainty assumptions. It helps distinguish a learned behavioural improvement from a mechanism intended to enforce constraints. Those guarantees depend on their assumptions and do not automatically apply to our latent predictions or monitor-only experiments. For another perspective, the same course provides Jason Choi's [Introduction to Control Lyapunov Functions and Control Barrier Functions](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_Tkn_Hzo4AA).

**Keep internal neural adapters as an optional branch.** Houlsby and colleagues' [Parameter-Efficient Transfer Learning for NLP](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v97/houlsby19a.html) explains small task-specific modules around frozen pretrained parameters. For low-rank updates, use the [LoRA paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.09685), the [inventor's explainer](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DhRoTONcyZE), linked by the [official repository](https://github.com/microsoft/LoRA), and the [PEFT conceptual guide](https://huggingface.co/docs/peft/main/en/conceptual_guides/lora). These explain compact parameterizations, not robotic safety. Inserting LoRA inside the world model would change its effective predictions even if its original tensors remain frozen. That would differ from our present intention to keep the world model's function fixed.

For a manageable first session, watch the two short MPC videos and the residual demonstration, then read the World Models article. Continue to POPLIN and LOOP when deciding where adaptation should enter the planner. Treat RMA as the conditioning alternative and safety filters as the comparison that clarifies what our safety claims would require.

As you read, record each method's inputs, outputs, frozen components, learning feedback and remaining human design choices. This makes “general adapter” precise: the same architecture with separately learned weights is one claim; the same weights working across tasks is another. Our immediate question is whether a common interface supplies enough useful control without repeated behavioural redesign. The literature gives us several credible candidates, but choosing among them still needs evidence from our systems.
