## Key question 1: Noise handling in EAs — which technique for which budget, and the simplest adequate one for 4–6 parameters and Bernoulli fitness

### Takeaway
The literature offers five families (resampling, adaptive/sequential resampling, racing/elimination, rank-based uncertainty handling inside CMA-ES, noise-aware NSGA-II variants) plus surrogates; the 2025 head-to-head evidence for noisy NSGA-II shows that plain static resampling remains competitive with every adaptive scheme, and the theory literature disagrees on whether resampling beats enlarging the population. For 4–6 parameters and 1,000–2,000 episodes the simplest defensible choice is rank-based selection (CMA-ES or NSGA-II) with a small fixed per-individual sample under common random numbers, optionally with pycma's built-in uncertainty handler; nothing more elaborate has a clear evidence advantage in this regime.

### Cited Findings
- Surveys that organise the field: Jin and Branke, "Evolutionary Optimization in Uncertain Environments—A Survey", IEEE TEVC 9(3):303–317, 2005, classify uncertainty into noisy fitness, robustness, approximated fitness and moving optima — [Bielefeld record](https://pub.uni-bielefeld.de/record/2978653) (record only; full text 404'd this session). Rakshit, Konar and Das, "Noisy evolutionary optimization algorithms – A comprehensive survey", Swarm and Evolutionary Computation 33:18–45, 2017, DOI 10.1016/j.swevo.2016.09.002, organise noisy EAs around five add-on strategies: fitness sampling, fitness estimation, dynamic population sizing, adapting the search strategy, and modifying the selection step — [ISI Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.isical.ac.in/journal-articles/2624).
- Explicit resampling: evaluate each solution k times and use the mean; k can be fixed or adapted during the run — [Roostapour et al. review, arXiv 1806.08547](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.08547). The standard error of a Bernoulli rate falls as sqrt(k): at p=0.3, SE = 0.46 (k=1), 0.23 (k=4), 0.16 (k=8), 0.09 (k=26), 0.045 (k=104) — own computation.
- Resampling versus bigger populations is a long-standing disagreement. Fitzpatrick and Grefenstette, "Genetic algorithms in noisy environments", Machine Learning 3:101–120, 1988, DOI 10.1007/BF00113893, found "more efficient search results from less accurate evaluations", i.e. in some cases spend the budget on more individuals rather than more samples per individual — [MLAnthology](https://mlanthology.org/mlj/1988/fitzpatrick1988mlj-genetic). Beyer, "Evolutionary algorithms in noisy environments: theoretical issues and guidelines for practice", CMAME 186(2):239–267, 2000 — [record](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~shivaram/readings/b2hd-Beyer2000.html) (record only); the Arnold–Beyer book *Noisy Optimization with Evolution Strategies* investigates "the benefits of distributed populations, and the potential of genetic repair" under noise — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/book/9781461353973). Qian, Yu and Zhou, "Analyzing Evolutionary Optimization in Noisy Environments", arXiv 1311.4987, prove noise is not always harmful and that, on the problem they study, re-evaluation and threshold selection "do not make the EA more noise tolerant" — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1311.4987). Against this, Hansen et al. (below) and Dinu et al. 2024 report adaptive re-evaluation helping CMA-ES.
- Hansen's CMA-ES tutorial (arXiv 1604.00772) gives default λ = 4 + ⌊3 ln n⌋ and μ = ⌊λ/2⌋, states "Increasing λ usually improves the global search capability" at a linear-at-most cost in convergence per evaluation, that "decreasing λ is not recommended", that a footnote notes λ ≥ 4n is needed for some settings, recommends "Independent restarts with increasing population size" (IPOP), and that CMA-ES is invariant to "order preserving (i.e. strictly monotonic) transformations of the objective function value" because it "only depends on the ranking of function values" — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1604.00772). For n=5 the default is λ=8, μ=4; for n=6, λ=9 — own arithmetic.
- UH-CMA-ES: Hansen, Niederberger, Guzzella and Koumoutsakos, "A method for handling uncertainty in evolutionary optimization with an application to feedback control of combustion", IEEE TEVC 13(1):180–197, 2009, DOI 10.1109/TEVC.2008.924423. It "relies on the rank based selection operator", is "independent of the uncertainty distribution", "prevents premature convergence", and "requires only a small number of additional function evaluations" — [IP Paris record](https://researchportal.ip-paris.fr/en/publications/a-method-for-handling-uncertainty-in-evolutionary-optimization-wi/). The operative criterion, as restated by later users: uncertainty is a problem for a rank-based algorithm "if and only if" noise-induced rank changes exceed the true differences — [search summary of citing papers](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1209.0616). It ships in pycma as `noise_handler=True` passed to `fmin2` (v3.4.0 interface); recommended citation Hansen, Akimoto, Baudis, CMA-ES/pycma on GitHub, Zenodo DOI 10.5281/zenodo.2559635 — [pycma README](https://github.com/CMA-ES/pycma).
- Newer adaptive re-evaluation for CMA-ES: Dinu, Patel, Bonet-Monroig and Wang, "An Adaptive Re-evaluation Method for Evolution Strategy under Additive Noise", arXiv 2409.16757 (v3, June 2025), choose the re-evaluation count per iteration by maximising a lower bound on expected improvement; they claim a "significant advantage in the probability of reaching near-optimal function values" over state-of-the-art noise handling, but the abstract names no budgets — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.16757).
- Racing: Maron and Moore, "Hoeffding Races: Accelerating Model Selection Search for Classification and Function Approximation", NIPS 6, pp. 59–66, 1993, discards weak candidates early and concentrates evaluations on separating the strongest — [NeurIPS proceedings](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/1993/hash/02a32ad2669e6fe298e607fe7cc0e1a0-Abstract.html). F-Race (Birattari, Stützle, Paquete, Varrentrapp, GECCO 2002) evaluates survivors on sampled instances, applies a Friedman test and eliminates inferior configurations with pairwise tests — [PDF](https://cs.ubc.ca/labs/algorithms/Courses/CPSC536H-10/Materials/BirEtAl02.pdf); iterated F-Race overview: Birattari, Yuan, Balaprakash, Stützle 2010 — [Springer](https://www.springerprofessional.de/en/f-race-and-iterated-f-race-an-overview/3124840). irace (López-Ibáñez, Dubois-Lacoste, Pérez Cáceres, Birattari, Stützle, Operations Research Perspectives 3:43–58, 2016) implements it with elitist racing — [Manchester record](https://research.manchester.ac.uk/en/publications/the-irace-package-iterated-racing-for-automatic-algorithm-configu/). irace defaults: `firstTest = 5` instances "before the first elimination test", `eachTest = 1`, test is the F-test (Friedman) unless capping, `confidence = 0.95`, `elitist = 1`, `mu = 5` — [irace reference](https://mlopez-ibanez.github.io/irace/reference/defaultScenario.html).
- Successive halving / Hyperband: Li, Jamieson, DeSalvo, Rostamizadeh and Talwalkar, "Hyperband: A Novel Bandit-Based Approach to Hyperparameter Optimization", JMLR 18:1–52, 2018, extends SuccessiveHalving (Jamieson and Talwalkar), which Karnin et al. 2013 originally proposed "for the stochastic setting"; Assumption 1 is that each arm's loss sequence converges to a limit ν_i as the resource grows, and the paper warns that when "the performance on a smaller resource is not indicative of that on a larger resource" the benefit of early stopping is muted — [arXiv / ar5iv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.06560).
- Noisy NSGA-II lineage (as catalogued in Budszuhn, Krallmann, Horn, "Adaptive Resampling with Bootstrap for Noisy Multi-Objective Optimization Problems", arXiv 2503.21495v2, April 2025): static resampling (N fixed); time-based, rank-based and domination-strength resampling (Siegmund, Ng, Deb 2013); standard-error dynamic resampling until SE < threshold (Di Pietro, While, Barone 2004); confidence-based dynamic resampling (Syberfeldt et al. 2010); probability of dominance (Teich 2001); Rolling Tide EA (Fieldsend and Everson). ARB's own experiments used 50,000 evaluations, population 40, 30 replications. Headline: ARB beat RTEA in 64.7% of settings and lost in 24.7%; but "a one-shot static strategy" was "the only strategy that outperformed ARB across all settings" (22.4% vs 20.7%), RTEA tended to win under Gaussian noise and static resampling under χ² noise, and the authors say the fractions "should be interpreted with caution" — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.21495v2).
- Confidence-based dynamic resampling: Syberfeldt, Ng, John, Moore, EJOR 204(3):533–544, 2010, DOI 10.1016/j.ejor.2009.11.003, keeps sampling a solution "until it is confident it is being ranked correctly", tested on ZDT and two manufacturing problems — [Falmouth repository](https://repository.falmouth.ac.uk/744/), [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ejores/v204y2010i3p533-544.html).
- Rolling Tide EA: Fieldsend and Everson, IEEE TEVC 19(1):103–117, 2015; strong elitism with repeated resampling of elite points, a reserved refinement budget, compared with eight EMO algorithms on 70 noisy instances of ten CEC'09 problems; code at github.com/fieldsend/ieee_tec_2014_rtea — [Exeter repository](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/handle/10871/17063).
- Goh and Tan, "An investigation on noisy environments in evolutionary multiobjective optimization", IEEE TEVC 11(3):354–381, 2007 — [CityU record](https://scholars.cityu.edu.hk/en/publications/an-investigation-on-noisy-environments-in-evolutionary-multi-obje/) (record only). Their 2006 conference precursor reports that optimizer output "degrades toward random search as noise rises".
- Surrogates and small budgets: Bartz-Beielstein et al. state "surrogate-assisted algorithms tend to be algorithms of choice for small budgets", while evolution strategies are more competitive at mid and large budgets — [arXiv 2007.03488](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.03488). The policy-search survey (Chatzilygeroudis, Vassiliades, Stulp, Calinon, Mouret, IEEE T-RO 2019/arXiv 1807.02303) says "most policy search algorithms require thousands of training episodes", that "BO is limited to low-dimensional policy spaces" with one example of "around 100 trials with a well-chosen 15D policy space", calls CMA-ES "one of the most successful algorithms for optimizing noisy and black-box functions", and recommends for "High-DOF robots with low-dimensional policies: Bayesian optimisation gives the best trade-off" — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.02303).
- pymoo's NSGA-II defaults: `pop_size=100`, tournament selection, SBX, polynomial mutation, rank-and-crowding survival; cites Deb, Pratap, Agarwal, Meyarivan, IEEE TEVC 6(2):182–197, 2002, DOI 10.1109/4235.996017 — [pymoo](https://pymoo.org/algorithms/moo/nsga2.html).

### Inferences
- Budget arithmetic (own computation): if every individual is evaluated on the full 26-task panel (26 episodes), 1,000 episodes buys 38 distinct individuals and 2,000 buys 76; at CMA-ES default λ=8 that is 4–9 generations, which is too few for covariance adaptation to matter. With k=8 episodes per individual, λ=8: 64 episodes/generation → 15 generations (1,000) or 31 (2,000). With k=4, λ=8: 31 or 62 generations. So the design decision is forced: evaluate on a small stratified sub-panel per individual, not the full panel.
- With Bernoulli outcomes at k=4–8, per-individual SE is 0.16–0.23, larger than most true differences between neighbours. What makes this workable is (a) rank-based selection only needs the top half enriched, not individually correct; (b) common random numbers across the generation (Key question 2) turn the comparison into a paired one; (c) elitism re-evaluated each generation (the RTEA/ARB principle: re-check elites rather than trust a lucky draw).
- Given ARB 2025's finding that static resampling was the only strategy beating ARB everywhere, and Qian et al.'s result that re-evaluation is not uniformly helpful, a reviewer cannot reasonably demand an adaptive resampler; static k with CRN plus elite re-evaluation is defensible and simplest.
- For the single-objective/scalarised runs (or the inner loop of a tiny controller), pycma with `noise_handler=True` is the least-machinery option that has a canonical citation (Hansen et al. 2009) and a maintained implementation; its cost is a few extra evaluations per iteration.
- Racing (irace-style F-test after 5 shared instances, or successive halving within a generation) is the right tool if the per-individual sample has to be very small: start all λ on 4 shared episodes, drop the bottom half, give survivors 4 more. It is well-cited but adds machinery; use it only if the static design visibly wastes budget on obvious losers.
- Surrogates/BO: reviewers from EMO/ENUM will ask "why not BO or a surrogate at 10³ evaluations?" (Bartz-Beielstein; Chatzilygeroudis). Answer in the paper: outcomes are categorical/Bernoulli with task-specific bias and two objectives; a BO baseline with matched budget (or an explicit justification) is advisable.

## Key question 2: Common random numbers and paired evaluation — evidence, seed panels, pitfalls

### Takeaway
CRN is standard in simulation optimisation and is guaranteed to reduce variance of differences under monotonicity/continuity conditions (Glasserman and Yao 1992); in population-based noisy optimisation it gave "by far most of the improvement" on a real problem but was harmful on some artificial noise models (Decock, Liu, Teytaud 2015). Fixed seed panels invite overfitting: RL agents trained on fixed level sets overfit "even with 16,000 training levels" (Cobbe et al.), so the search panel must be separated from a held-out confirmation panel, and the benchmarking guidelines explicitly recommend paired designs with shared seeds.

### Cited Findings
- Glasserman and Yao, "Some Guidelines and Guarantees for Common Random Numbers", Management Science 38(6):884–908, 1992, examine "the effectiveness and optimality of CRN in a general setting, stressing the roles played by monotonicity and continuity properties" and identify classes of systems where CRN is "beneficial and even optimal"; they condition on event timing rather than state sequences — [INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.38.6.884), [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v38y1992i6p884-908.html). A secondary source overstates this as a general guarantee — [arXiv 1305.7424](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1305.7424); the guarantee holds only under the stated conditions.
- Decock, Liu and Teytaud, "Variance reduction in population-based optimization: Application to unit commitment", GECCO 2015 Companion, pp. 1377–1378, DOI 10.1145/2739482.2764638: CRN "performed very well on some artificial noise models" and was "detrimental on other artificial noise models"; on the unit-commitment problem "CRN gave by far most of the improvement"; stratified sampling "is never detrimental" — [Lingnan record](https://scholars.ln.edu.hk/en/publications/variance-reduction-in-population-based-optimization-application-t/).
- Pearce, Poloczek and Branke, "Bayesian Optimization Allowing for Common Random Numbers", arXiv 1910.09259 (2019; journal version in Operations Research, not verified online): reusing a seed "lets users compare inputs under the same randomly generated scenario"; they model per-seed outputs jointly and choose (input, seed) pairs — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.09259). Search-summary of the paper: "CRN typically induces a positive correlation in noise, which reduces the variance of performance differences" — [search result](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.09259).
- Bartz-Beielstein et al. 2020, Section 6: "For paired samples, use the same seeds across algorithms", "CRNs might reduce the variances and lead to more reliable statistical conclusions", and choose paired vs unpaired by design; C4.5 recommends "letting all candidate algorithms use the same starting points" — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2007.03488).
- Seed-reuse pitfall: CRN "relies on a critical assumption: that the same draw index corresponds to the same modeled event across scenarios. Stateful pseudorandom number generators (PRNGs) violate this assumption" — [Buffalo, Pearson, Klein 2026, arXiv 2603.11084](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.11084). The user's simulator reproduces scene layout exactly from the seed, which satisfies this for the scene; policy-side stochasticity (sampling temperature, if any) must also be keyed to the seed.
- Overfitting to a fixed seed panel: Cobbe, Klimov, Hesse, Kim, Schulman, "Quantifying Generalization in Reinforcement Learning", arXiv 1812.02341 (ICML 2019): with 100 training levels, train score 99.4 vs test 66.6 (Nature-CNN); "substantial overfitting occurs when there are less than 4,000 training levels"; "Even with 16,000 training levels, overfitting is still noticeable"; agents "perform best when trained on an unbounded set of levels" — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.02341), [ar5iv table](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1812.02341).
- ES in RL uses CRN for communication of perturbations: Salimans, Ho, Chen, Sidor, Sutskever, "Evolution Strategies as a Scalable Alternative to Reinforcement Learning", arXiv 1703.03864 — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.03864).
- Reproducibility guidance: "One should document or provide as artifacts the precise random seeds that produce the results reported", yet "the conclusions should not depend on the precise seeds and a reproducibility study should vary the seeds" — López-Ibáñez, Branke, Paquete, "Reproducibility in Evolutionary Computation", ACM TELO 2021, DOI 10.1145/3466624 — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2102.03380).

### Inferences
- Paired-design gain (own computation, 0.23 vs 0.33 safe-success, 80% power, α=0.05 two-sided): unpaired needs 313–316 episodes per arm; with CRN-induced outcome correlation ρ=0.2 the paired requirement drops to 251, ρ=0.3 → 220, ρ=0.5 → 158. The user's "about 200 episodes per condition" estimate corresponds to ρ≈0.3 under pairing, or to a larger effect (0.23→0.38 needs 147 unpaired). The measured fact that same-seed repeats disagree on half of 26 tasks means ρ is modest, so expect gains of 20–30%, not a free lunch.
- Fixed seed panel per generation (all λ individuals see the same tasks and seeds) is the EA analogue of "same starting points" and costs nothing; rotate the panel across generations (a fresh draw from the search seed pool each generation) so that the population does not specialise to one layout. This is the simplest implementation of the Cobbe lesson.
- Partition seeds before the study: a search pool (used for all selection decisions) and a held-out confirmation pool (never seen by the optimiser), with the final Pareto points re-evaluated only on the held-out pool. Report both numbers; the search-pool number is the "training" rate.
- Because CRN can hurt under some noise models (Decock et al.), a one-paragraph sanity check — the pairing correlation observed on the baseline — is cheap insurance against a reviewer asking "did pairing help?"

### Gaps
- No evolutionary-robotics paper was found that quantifies overfitting to a fixed seed panel for a 4–6-parameter wrapper; the Cobbe numbers are for deep RL policies with far more capacity, so the overfitting risk for 4–6 parameters is probably much smaller, but no citation supports that directly.
- The Pearce et al. journal version (Operations Research 70(6), 2022) was not verified online this session.

## Key question 3: Early termination, cheap proxies, multi-fidelity — what the literature says about bias

### Takeaway
Early stopping is justified in the bandit literature only under an explicit assumption that low-budget performance converges to and ranks like full-budget performance (Hyperband Assumption 1); multi-fidelity BO treats cheap approximations as biased, correlated proxies rather than unbiased estimates; evolutionary robotics has a GECCO precedent for adapting evaluation length. Stopping at the first violation is exact for the user's "violation" and "safe success" labels and only censors the unsafe-success/unsafe-failure distinction and the time-to-success of violating episodes.

### Cited Findings
- Hyperband's formal basis: "For each i the limit lim_{k→∞} ℓ_{i,k} exists and is equal to ν_i" (Assumption 1), with an envelope function γ bounding deviation from the limit; the paper notes cases where "the performance on a smaller resource is not indicative of that on a larger resource" and that then "the benefit of early-stopping can be muted" — [ar5iv 1603.06560](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1603.06560).
- Multi-fidelity BO with continuous approximations (Kandasamy, Dasarathy, Schneider, Póczos, arXiv 1703.06240, ICML 2017) uses cheaper approximations (less data, fewer iterations) to speed up optimisation; the approximations are modelled as a continuous fidelity space rather than as unbiased estimates — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06240).
- Evolutionary robotics precedent for adaptive evaluation length: Dinu, Dimitrov, Weel, Eiben, "Self-adapting fitness evaluation times for on-line evolution of simulated robots", GECCO 2013, pp. 191–198 — [VU record](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/self-adapting-fitness-evaluation-times-for-on-line-evolution-of-s/) (record only; abstract not available).
- Racing is itself a form of early termination at the level of candidates rather than episodes: irace eliminates after `firstTest = 5` shared instances using the Friedman test at 95% confidence — [irace reference](https://mlopez-ibanez.github.io/irace/reference/defaultScenario.html); F-Race discards candidates "once enough statistical evidence accumulates against them" — [Birattari et al. 2002](https://cs.ubc.ca/labs/algorithms/Courses/CPSC536H-10/Materials/BirEtAl02.pdf).
- Simulator fidelity: Mouret and Chatzilygeroudis, "20 Years of Reality Gap: a few Thoughts about Simulators in Evolutionary Robotics", GECCO 2017 Companion, pp. 1121–1124, argue most ER experiments still run in simulation and that simulators should "estimate their own confidence in fitness predictions" — [HAL](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-01518764). The policy-search survey notes "physics simulators are accurate for some policies, e.g., static gaits, and inaccurate for some others" and that one can "learn in simulation if the search is constrained to policies that are simulated accurately" — [arXiv 1807.02303](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.02303).

### Inferences
- Stopping an episode at the first constraint violation: the "violated" flag is observed exactly, and "safe success" requires no violation, so both headline rates (safe-success rate, violation rate) are unbiased under early stopping. What is lost is (a) whether the violating episode would have succeeded (unsafe-success vs unsafe-failure), and (b) its completion time. If the second objective is "time to safe success" (violating episodes scored at horizon), nothing is biased; if the paper wants to report all four classes, run the confirmation panel without early stopping.
- Short-horizon screening: success-by-T is monotone non-decreasing in T, so a short-horizon success rate is a lower bound on the full-horizon rate (one-sided, conservative bias). That matches Hyperband's convergence assumption, but it also means a reliable-but-slow wrapper setting is systematically under-ranked at short horizon. If speed is one of the two objectives, this bias is simply the objective; if not, use short horizons only for elimination of clear losers and confirm survivors at full horizon.
- The mandatory reviewer-proofing step is a calibration figure: the short-horizon (or early-stopped) estimate against the full-horizon estimate on the same seeds for the baseline and two or three evolved points, showing rank preservation. This is the empirical check of the Hyperband assumption and costs a few dozen episodes.

### Gaps
- No paper was found that quantifies bias from truncating robot episodes at the first safety violation; the argument above is first-principles, not cited.
- A dedicated survey of multi-fidelity evolutionary optimisation (as opposed to BO) was not located within the search quota.

## Key question 4: Statistics for success rates and Pareto fronts, and the benchmarking guidelines

### Takeaway
For binary outcomes use Wilson (or Agresti–Coull/Jeffreys) intervals rather than Wald, Fisher's exact test for unpaired and McNemar for paired comparisons; reaching 80% power for a ten-point change in a 23% rate needs roughly 300 episodes per arm unpaired and 220 paired at ρ=0.3 (own computation). The benchmarking guidelines (Bartz-Beielstein et al. 2020; López-Ibáñez et al. 2021; Derrac et al. 2011) prescribe non-parametric tests with Holm-type correction when comparing across problems, prefer confidence intervals and effect sizes over bare p-values, and give no fixed number of runs; the "30 runs" figure is a convention, not a rule. For Pareto fronts, hypervolume over replications with bootstrap intervals plus attainment-surface plots is the accepted pair.

### Cited Findings
- Binomial intervals: Brown, Cai and DasGupta, "Interval Estimation for a Binomial Proportion", Statistical Science 16(2):101–133, 2001, DOI 10.1214/ss/1009213286, show the Wald interval "cannot be trusted" and recommend "the Wilson interval or the equal-tailed Jeffreys prior interval for small n and the interval suggested in Agresti and Coull for larger n" — [Project Euclid](https://projecteuclid.org/euclid.ss/1009213286), [ASAIP summary](https://asaip.psu.edu/?p=677). Wilson 95% intervals at the user's scale (own computation): 24/104 = 0.231 → [0.160, 0.320]; 43/104 = 0.413 → [0.324, 0.510]; 6/26 → [0.110, 0.421]; 46/200 → [0.177, 0.293].
- Power (own computation, two-proportion z-test, α=0.05 two-sided, 80% power, per arm): 0.23 vs 0.33 → 316; 0.23 vs 0.38 → 147; 0.23 vs 0.43 → 86; 0.41 vs 0.31 → 361; 0.41 vs 0.26 → 155; 0.41 vs 0.21 → 83. Power at n=200 per arm: 0.61 for 0.23→0.33, 0.91 for 0.23→0.38, 0.55 for 0.41→0.31, 0.89 for 0.41→0.26. At n=104 per arm power is 0.32–0.36 for ten-point changes.
- Non-parametric tests across problems: Derrac, García, Molina, Herrera, "A practical tutorial on the use of nonparametric statistical tests as a methodology for comparing evolutionary and swarm intelligence algorithms", Swarm and Evolutionary Computation 1(1):3–18, 2011, DOI 10.1016/j.swevo.2011.02.002, survey pairwise and multiple-comparison procedures, motivated by parametric assumptions being violated in computational-intelligence studies — [PDF](https://sci2s.ugr.es/sites/default/files/files/TematicWebSites/sicidm/2011-Derrac-SWEVO.pdf).
- Bartz-Beielstein, Doerr, Berg, Bossek, Chandrasekaran, Eftimov, Fischbach, Kerschke, La Cava, López-Ibáñez, Malan, Moore, Naujoks, Orzechowski, Volz, Wagner, Weise, "Benchmarking in Optimization: Best Practice and Open Issues", arXiv 2007.03488 (v2, Dec 2020): single problem/two algorithms → t-test or Mann–Whitney U; multiple problems/two algorithms → Wilcoxon signed-rank; more than two → Friedman/Iman–Davenport with Holm, Nemenyi, Shaffer or Bergmann post hoc; "pairwise tests in place of a post-hoc procedure" loses family-wise error control; check normality and variance assumptions first; "Statistical significance does not imply practical meaning"; "Pair every 'A outperforms B' claim with suitable statistical test results"; relying on means "sensitive to outliers from poor runs" is a named mistake; no fixed run count — design should decide "which and how many algorithm runs should be performed"; report both function evaluations and CPU time; tune before benchmarking or "compare against default parameters and state this limitation"; prefer factorial/space-filling designs over one-factor-at-a-time; "Report negative results"; share code, data and containers; preregistration is suggested — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2007.03488). The paper also flags that "real-world benchmarks are often single-shot optimizations" so sometimes only one run can be conducted, and that the per-algorithm budget "is typically driven by the research question or application of interest".
- López-Ibáñez, Branke, Paquete 2021: authors should report "not only means and variances, but also confidence intervals", with confidence intervals preferred over p-values and effect sizes recommended; non-parametric methods favoured; artifacts "should contain, at a minimum, all the source code and the input data required to reproduce", raw data should include "the actual solutions", repositories should have a DOI (Zenodo), personal webpages "do not typically satisfy this requirement", and results should not be reported "with a confidence or precision larger than what can actually be reproduced" — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2102.03380).
- Number of seeds in the RL literature (frequently cited by ER reviewers): Henderson et al., "Deep Reinforcement Learning that Matters", AAAI 2018, show two groups of 5 seeds of the same TRPO configuration differing with "t=−9.0916, p=0.0016", state "there can be no specific number of trials specified as a recommendation", and use 10k-iteration bootstrap intervals — [ar5iv 1709.06560](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1709.06560). Colas, Sigaud, Oudeyer, "How Many Random Seeds?", arXiv 1806.08295: pilot with n=5 gave β=0.51; "To meet the requirement β=0.2, N should be increased to N=10"; recommend "Use the Welch's t-test over the bootstrap confidence interval test", "Use at least n=20 samples in the pilot study", "Use larger sample size N than the one prescribed by the power analysis", and lower α when running many comparisons — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1806.08295). Agarwal, Schwarzer, Castro, Courville, Bellemare, "Deep RL at the Edge of the Statistical Precipice", NeurIPS 2021 (Outstanding Paper), recommend interval estimates, performance profiles and the interquartile mean, with the `rliable` library — [arXiv 2108.13264](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.13264).
- "30 replicates ... then 100 or more" is a 2013 blog convention (Olson), not a guideline — [blog](https://www.randalolson.com/2013/07/20/a-short-guide-to-using-statistics-in-evolutionary-computation/).
- Pareto-front assessment: Zitzler, Thiele, Laumanns, Fonseca, Grunert da Fonseca, "Performance Assessment of Multiobjective Optimizers: An Analysis and Review", IEEE TEVC 7(2):117–132, 2003 — [PDF](https://sci2s.ugr.es/sites/default/files/files/Teaching/OtherPostGraduateCourses/FutureDirectionsinSoftComputing/Bibliografia/Zitzler_Assessment.pdf); Knowles, Thiele, Zitzler, "A Tutorial on the Performance Assessment of Stochastic Multiobjective Optimizers", TIK-Report 214, ETH Zurich, Feb 2006 — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/1830761.1830834) (record only). Empirical attainment functions: López-Ibáñez, Paquete, Stützle, "Exploratory Analysis of Stochastic Local Search Algorithms in Biobjective Optimization", in *Experimental Methods for the Analysis of Optimization Algorithms*, Springer 2010, pp. 209–222, DOI 10.1007/978-3-642-02538-9_9; the `eaf` package "implements plots of summary attainment surfaces and differences between the first-order EAFs" and the EAF "describes the probabilistic distribution of the outcomes obtained by a stochastic algorithm in the objective space" — [eaf docs](https://mlopez-ibanez.github.io/eaf/). ARB 2025 used dominated hypervolume over 30 replications and reports IGD "gave no substantially different results" — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.21495v2).
- GECCO 2025 tutorials on method: "Benchmarking single- and multi-objective optimization algorithms" (Doerr, Vermetten, de Nobel, Bäck); "Fair Performance Comparison of Evolutionary Multi-Objective Algorithms" (Ishibuchi, Pang); "Evolutionary computation for stochastic problems" (F. Neumann, A. Neumann, Singh) — [GECCO 2025 tutorials](https://gecco-2025.sigevo.org/Tutorials).

### Inferences
- The right unit of statistical inference here is the episode, not the EA run: the claim "operating point A has a higher safe-success rate than baseline B" is a two-proportion comparison on the held-out panel. Use McNemar (paired by seed, the CRN design) or Fisher's exact (unpaired), report Wilson intervals on each rate and a CI on the difference, and state the pre-specified minimum effect (ten points) with the power computed above. That satisfies Bartz-Beielstein's "pair every claim with a test" and López-Ibáñez's "report confidence intervals".
- Replicating the EA 30 times is infeasible (30 × 2,000 episodes ≈ 60,000 episodes at 2–4 GPU-min each ≈ 2,000–4,000 GPU-hours); 3–5 independent EA runs with all fronts shown, plus EAF/attainment summary, plus per-point confirmation on 200 held-out episodes, is consistent with the GECCO companion precedent of five runs (Key question 5) and with the RL literature's shift from run counts to interval estimates. Say explicitly that the number of runs was budget-limited and that per-run fronts are shown rather than only an aggregate.
- Hypervolume CI: with 3–5 runs a bootstrap over runs is degenerate; prefer (a) per-run hypervolume listed individually, (b) hypervolume of the confirmation-panel re-evaluated front with a bootstrap over episodes (resample the held-out episodes, recompute the rates of each front point, recompute HV), which is well-defined and uses the large sample where it exists.
- McNemar (1947, Psychometrika 12:153–157) and Fisher's exact test are textbook; not re-verified online this session.

### Gaps
- Could not verify the CEC competition run-count conventions (25 runs in CEC 2005, 51 in later CEC real-parameter competitions) online this session; cited from memory only, so treat "30 runs" as folklore unless a venue rule is found.
- The Knowles–Thiele–Zitzler tutorial's content (its recommended test for hypervolume differences) was not fetched.
- Fonseca and Fleming 1996 (PPSN IV, attainment surfaces) not verified online; cite via López-Ibáñez et al. 2010 if needed.

## Key question 5: GECCO norms for evolutionary robotics / real-world application papers

### Takeaway
GECCO 2026 rules (the closest available proxy for 2027): 8 pages excluding references, double-blind, ≤10 MB anonymised supplementary material with Zenodo recommended, and review criteria that include "whether there is enough information to permit replication". The RWA track accepts only "strictly new real-world applications" and "will not consider articles that use previously existing, publicly available real-world benchmark problems", which affects track choice. Sim-only ER work is routine (a 2026 GECCO Companion paper uses ARGoS with five independent runs, 20 re-test trials per controller, Mann–Whitney U with Holm–Bonferroni), and recent ER-adjacent preprints with three seeds explicitly disclaim significance claims — the honest framing reviewers accept.

### Cited Findings
- GECCO 2026 call for papers: full papers "at most 8 pages (excluding references)"; poster-only 4 pages; "Reviewing is double-blind, so submissions must be anonymized" including citing one's own work in third person and anonymising supplementary material (LaTeX `anonymous=true,review=true`); supplementary material "may include videos, a PDF appendix, source code, data, or proofs", must be PDF/ZIP/tarball ≤10 MB, "Reviewers may or may not consider supplementary material", a permanent public repository such as Zenodo is suggested; reviewers evaluate "significance, technical soundness, novelty, clarity, writing quality, relevance, and whether there is enough information to permit replication"; 2026 deadlines were abstract 19 Jan, paper 26 Jan (non-extensible), notification 20 Mar — [GECCO 2026 CFP](https://gecco-2026.sigevo.org/Call-for-Papers).
- GECCO 2027: Kraków, Poland, 12–16 July 2027, "in person only"; the CFP, deadlines and track list are not yet posted (contact address inconsistently links to gecco2026@sigevo.org) — [GECCO 2027 site](https://gecco-2027.sigevo.org/).
- GECCO 2026 tracks and chairs: RWA (Brownlee, Kalkreuth, Mei); EMO (Binois, Hernandez Castellanos); NE (Wilson, Gagné); ENUM (Auger, Arnold); GA (Thierens, Coello Coello); EML (Urbanowicz, Browne); no track named Evolutionary Robotics — [Tracks](https://gecco-2026.sigevo.org/Tracks).
- RWA track scope: "welcomes rigorous experimental, computational and/or applied advances in evolutionary computation (EC)"; it will "handle strictly new real-world applications", meaning problems "newly modelled and solved with evolutionary algorithms"; "we will not consider articles that use previously existing, publicly available real-world benchmark problems" (such papers are directed to other tracks); multidisciplinary papers should be readable by "researchers from other fields" — [RWA track page](https://gecco-2026.sigevo.org/Track?itemId=60).
- Sim-only ER example in GECCO 2026 Companion: Leopardi, Hamann, Kuckling, Kaiser, "The Evaluation Cost of Task Specialization in Evolutionary Multi-Robot Systems", arXiv 2606.24191, DOI 10.1145/3795101.3805286: ARGoS simulator, population 96, tournament size 5, 5,000 generations, "five evolutionary independent runs per behavior and subteam size", best controllers re-tested "in 20 random trials", 60 s episodes, Mann–Whitney U with Holm–Bonferroni correction, no runtime/hardware reported — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.24191), [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.24191v1).
- Small-seed ER-adjacent example (2026 preprint, venue unstated): EvoGym soft-robot co-design with budgets of 250–1,000 morphology evaluations (each a full PPO training), population 25, three seeds (0, 9, 42), results "mean ± s.d. over three seeds", and the explicit statement that reported values "should not be interpreted as formal statistical-significance claims" but as "descriptive comparisons under a fixed evaluation protocol" — [arXiv 2605.25832](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.25832).
- Simulation framing: Mouret and Chatzilygeroudis (GECCO 2017 Companion) note that "most evolutionary robotics experiments still run in simulation after 20+ years" — [HAL](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-01518764).
- "Why not RL" ammunition: Mania, Guy, Recht, "Simple random search provides a competitive approach to reinforcement learning", arXiv 1803.07055, match "state-of-the-art sample efficiency on the benchmark MuJoCo locomotion tasks" with linear policies, are "at least 15 times more efficient than the fastest competing model-free methods", evaluate over "hundreds of random seeds" and report "high variability in performance" — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.07055). Salimans et al. 2017 present ES as "a viable solution strategy" to MDP-based RL — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.03864). Chatzilygeroudis et al.: "Most policy search algorithms require thousands of training episodes" — [arXiv 1807.02303](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.02303).
- "Why not BO/surrogate" exposure: Bartz-Beielstein et al.: "surrogate-assisted algorithms tend to be algorithms of choice for small budgets" — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2007.03488); Chatzilygeroudis et al.: for "High-DOF robots with low-dimensional policies: Bayesian optimisation gives the best trade-off" — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.02303).
- Baselines per the guidelines: Bartz-Beielstein Sec. 4.1 lists one-shot methods "(e.g., pure random search)", local search, population-based and surrogate-based methods as candidate comparators; "The practitioner should be certain to compare versus the best algorithms"; comparing tuned vs untuned algorithms "are not fair and should be avoided" — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2007.03488). Henderson et al.: baselines "should match the original codebase results if available" — [ar5iv 1709.06560](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1709.06560).

### Inferences
- Track choice: if the simulator/benchmark the VLA policy is evaluated on is a publicly available benchmark, the RWA track's stated exclusion is a risk; EMO (Pareto dial, noisy objectives) or NE (if the tiny controller is the contribution) look safer, with ENUM plausible for a CMA-ES-centred methodological framing. The problem statement should be written to fit the chosen track's vocabulary.
- Mandatory baselines implied by the guidelines and by reviewer habits: (1) the unmodified frozen policy (hand-tuned/default wrapper), (2) random search at the identical episode budget and identical seed panels, (3) a grid or Latin-hypercube one-shot design of the same size (Bartz-Beielstein's "simple space-filling design with few points and repeats"), and ideally (4) a BO baseline or a sentence explaining why it is out of scope. Random search is the one reviewers will miss most, and it is almost free since it reuses the evaluation harness.
- Reporting template that matches the review criteria: episode budget per method, number of EA runs, seeds (search pool and held-out pool) released, wrapper parameter domains, simulator version and GPU type, wall-clock per episode, all code and raw per-episode outcome tables on Zenodo with a DOI, scripts that regenerate every figure.
- Presenting the Pareto front as a practical dial: order the non-dominated points by the safety objective, show the held-out safe-success and violation rates with Wilson intervals at each point, and mark the baseline; a monotone trade-off with non-overlapping intervals at the extremes is the "dial" claim, and it is testable point by point with McNemar against the baseline.
- Common criticisms to pre-empt in the text: "too few runs" (answer: budget-limited, all runs shown, inference on held-out episodes with power stated); "no statistical test" (McNemar/Fisher + CIs); "why not RL" (Mania; Salimans; frozen policy, 4–6 parameters); "why not BO" (binary two-objective outcomes; include or justify); "sim-only" (state it as the scope, cite the ER precedent, and report that seeds reproduce layouts exactly so results are reproducible).

### Gaps
- A systematic survey of 2023–2026 GECCO ER full papers' evaluation budgets could not be completed under the search quota; only two 2026 examples (ARGoS with 5 runs; EvoGym with 3 seeds) were verified. Typical cheap-simulator ER budgets (10^5–10^7 evaluations) are from memory and unverified.
- GECCO 2027's own CFP, page limit and track list are not yet published; the 2026 rules are used as proxy.
- No written GECCO policy on minimum run counts or mandatory baselines exists in the pages fetched; the norms above are inferred from the guidelines papers and examples.

## Key question 6: Published guidance for ~1,000–2,000 evaluations with 4–6 parameters and noisy fitness

### Takeaway
No source gives a population/generation recipe for this exact regime. The defaults that exist (CMA-ES λ = 4 + ⌊3 ln n⌋ = 8–9 for n = 5–6; pymoo NSGA-II `pop_size=100`) were set for noise-free evaluations; Hansen's advice is that λ may be increased but not decreased and that restarts with increasing population are a good general policy. The budget arithmetic, not the literature, fixes the design: a 1,000–2,000 episode budget supports at most a few hundred distinct individuals, so population size times per-individual samples must be chosen to leave 20–60 generations and to reserve roughly 600–800 episodes for confirmation.

### Cited Findings
- CMA-ES defaults and population advice (λ = 4 + ⌊3 ln n⌋, μ = ⌊λ/2⌋; "Increasing λ usually improves the global search capability"; "decreasing λ is not recommended"; IPOP restarts) — [ar5iv 1604.00772](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1604.00772).
- NSGA-II default `pop_size=100` in pymoo with no guidance on choosing it or on noise — [pymoo](https://pymoo.org/algorithms/moo/nsga2.html). ARB 2025 used population 40 with 50,000 evaluations — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.21495v2). Leopardi et al. 2026 used population 96 for 5,000 generations in a cheap simulator — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.24191v1). The EvoGym co-design preprint used 25 per generation for 10–40 generations when each evaluation was expensive — [arXiv 2605.25832](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.25832).
- Bartz-Beielstein et al.: budget "is typically driven by the research question or application of interest"; small budgets favour surrogate-assisted methods — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2007.03488).
- Chatzilygeroudis et al.: Bayesian optimisation handled "around 100 trials with a well-chosen 15D policy space" on a quadruped — [arXiv 1807.02303](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.02303).

### Inferences
- Budget table (own computation): B = 2,000 with λ = 8 and k = 8 episodes per individual → 31 generations and 248 distinct individuals; λ = 8, k = 4 → 62 generations; λ = 16, k = 4 → 31 generations; λ = 8, k = 26 (full panel) → 9 generations. B = 1,000 halves all of these. For NSGA-II, population 16–24 with k = 4–6 gives 8–15 generations at 1,000 episodes and 16–30 at 2,000 — thin but workable for 4–6 parameters when the initial population is a space-filling design.
- Reserve the confirmation budget first: 3–4 selected front points plus the baseline at 150–200 held-out episodes each is 600–1,000 episodes. Under a 2,000-episode ceiling that leaves ~1,000–1,400 for search. Writing the paper around "search budget" and "confirmation budget" as separate line items pre-empts the "too few evaluations" critique and matches the irace/RTEA practice of a reserved refinement budget.
- With expensive episodes and a cheap simulator being unavailable, the EvoGym preprint's pattern (population ~25, tens of generations, three seeds, descriptive claims plus a fixed protocol) is the closest published analogue; the ARGoS paper's pattern (five runs, 20 re-tests, Mann–Whitney + Holm) is the stronger template if five EA runs are affordable.

### Gaps
- No published study tunes population size or resampling for Bernoulli fitness at ~10^3 evaluations; the recommendations above are arithmetic plus the cited defaults.
- Whether CMA-ES's covariance adaptation contributes anything within 15–30 generations in 4–6 dimensions is not established by any fetched source; a random-search and a (1+1)-style or separable-CMA control would show it empirically.

## Simplest adequate protocol

### Takeaway
The least machinery reviewers would accept, assembled only from the practices cited above: rank-based optimiser with default or slightly enlarged population; a small fixed per-individual sample under common random numbers with a rotating per-generation seed panel; elites re-evaluated every generation; a strictly separate held-out seed pool for confirmation; random search and the untouched baseline at matched budget; Wilson intervals, McNemar (paired) or Fisher (unpaired), one pre-specified minimum effect with its power; per-run fronts shown plus an attainment summary; seeds, code and per-episode outcome tables on Zenodo.

### Cited Findings
- Rank-based selection with uncertainty handling is the canonical noise approach in CMA-ES (Hansen et al. 2009; pycma `noise_handler=True`) — [IP Paris record](https://researchportal.ip-paris.fr/en/publications/a-method-for-handling-uncertainty-in-evolutionary-optimization-wi/), [pycma](https://github.com/CMA-ES/pycma). Static resampling remains competitive with every adaptive scheme in the 2025 noisy-NSGA-II comparison — [arXiv 2503.21495](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.21495v2).
- Same seeds across compared algorithms ("paired samples") and same starting points are recommended practice — [Bartz-Beielstein et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2007.03488); seeds must be documented, conclusions must not depend on them — [López-Ibáñez et al. 2021](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2102.03380).
- Fixed training sets overfit; held-out evaluation is the remedy — [Cobbe et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.02341).
- Pure random search is a listed comparator; compare against tuned or default-stated baselines — [Bartz-Beielstein et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2007.03488); random search is competitive with model-free RL — [Mania et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.07055).
- Wilson/Agresti–Coull rather than Wald — [Brown, Cai, DasGupta](https://projecteuclid.org/euclid.ss/1009213286); confidence intervals and effect sizes over p-values — [López-Ibáñez et al. 2021](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2102.03380); power analysis before claiming a difference — [Colas et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1806.08295).
- Five independent runs, 20 re-tests, Mann–Whitney with Holm is an accepted sim-only ER pattern at GECCO 2026 Companion — [Leopardi et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.24191v1); attainment surfaces and EAF differences for stochastic multi-objective optimisers — [eaf docs](https://mlopez-ibanez.github.io/eaf/).
- 8 pages excluding references, double-blind, ≤10 MB anonymised supplement, Zenodo, replication as a review criterion — [GECCO 2026 CFP](https://gecco-2026.sigevo.org/Call-for-Papers).

### Inferences
- Concrete protocol for a 2,000-episode ceiling (own synthesis): (1) Split seeds into a search pool and a held-out pool before any run. (2) Optimiser: CMA-ES (pycma, λ = 8–12, `noise_handler=True`) on a scalarised safety-first objective, or NSGA-II with population 16–24 for the two-objective front; initial population from a Latin-hypercube design. (3) Each generation draws one panel of k = 4–8 (task, seed) pairs from the search pool, stratified over task groups; every individual in that generation is evaluated on exactly that panel; elites carried over are re-evaluated on the new panel and their running mean is used. (4) Early-stop episodes at first violation during search; confirmation episodes run to the full horizon. (5) Stop when the search budget (≈1,200) is spent; take 3–4 non-dominated points spanning the dial plus the baseline and evaluate each on 150–200 held-out episodes (≈800). (6) Statistics: Wilson intervals on each rate; McNemar on the paired held-out outcomes for each point versus baseline; report the pre-specified ten-point minimum effect and its power; show all EA runs' fronts and the EAF summary; hypervolume of the confirmed front with a bootstrap over held-out episodes. (7) Baselines at matched budget: untouched policy, random search over the same parameter box on the same panels, and a one-shot LHS design; add a BO baseline or one sentence of justification. (8) Release seeds, code, raw per-episode class labels, and figure scripts under a Zenodo DOI; report GPU type and minutes per episode.
- Everything in this list is a single mechanism with a canonical citation; the only optional extras are irace-style racing inside a generation (if obvious losers consume too much budget) and an adaptive resampler (no evidence advantage in the 2025 comparison). Leave both out unless the pilot shows need.

### Gaps
- The "simplest adequate" judgement is a synthesis; no single source certifies this exact protocol, and GECCO 2027 reviewing instructions are not yet published.
