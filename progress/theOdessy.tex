\section*{Task 1: Replicate the ManiGuard Jar benchmark}

\textbf{Why.} Before trying to improve the Jar policy we needed to know its real base rate, and whether our setup even matches the paper. The old runs never could: they used a different render profile (640-pixel high fidelity instead of the paper's 256-pixel legacy) on two tasks.

\textbf{What we did.}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
  \item Pinned ManiGuard to the last revision that still renders the paper's profile, and ran the paper's protocol exactly: 26 Jar tasks, 2{,}000 steps, 8 of 16 actions, official evaluator.
  \item Rented one two-GPU machine on Vast and ran seeds 1 and 2, then seed 0 twice (one per GPU) to see how much a repeated seed moves.
  \item Dropped an earlier seed 0 run from a different machine so that everything of record comes from one host. With it the test still passed; without it the safe-success rate goes from 15\% to 23\%.
  \item Saved every rollout video to a private Hugging Face dataset so anyone can watch what the robot did. Total cost about \$6.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Result.} Counts over 26 tasks per run. The paper's row is for comparison.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrr}
\toprule
 & Safe success & Unsafe success & Safe fail & Unsafe fail & Violations \\
\midrule
Seed 0, repeat 1 & 7 & 0 & 9 & 10 & 10 \\
Seed 0, repeat 2 & 7 & 5 & 7 & 7 & 12 \\
Seed 1 & 6 & 0 & 10 & 10 & 10 \\

\midrule
Ours, 104 episodes & 24 (23\%) & 7 & 37 & 36 & 43 (41\%) \\

Fisher tests against the paper: $p = 0.60$ for safe success, $p = 0.76$ for violations. Our setup matches the paper.

\textbf{What we learned.}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
  \item Running the same seed twice gives the same outcome on only 13 of 26 tasks, about the same as two different seeds. The robot reaches the jar the same way every time and then the physics at contact decides. One episode is a coin flip with a per-task bias.
  \item 13 tasks never succeed safely, 12 sit in the middle, one always succeeds. The middle 12 are where a dial could show anything.
  \item Most violations are the jar tipping over (33 of 43); the gripper only closed on the jar in 32 of 104 episodes.
  \item To tell a ten-point improvement from noise we will need about 200 episodes per condition. Everything after this is sized on that.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Where it lives.} Videos and rows: \texttt{Sachioster/maniguard-jar-replication-20261022} (private). Receipts: \texttt{progress/receipts/}. Full write-up: \emph{Jar replication and noise floor} under \texttt{docs/research/research\_notes/}.

\section*{Task 2: Look at what actually happened}

\textbf{Why.} Before trying any fix we wanted the 104 episodes to tell us where the leverage is, without assuming the answer. One rule for this task: describe, don't intervene.

\textbf{What we did.} An agent on a RunPod pod turned every episode into a timeline from the monitor logs (first contact, lid closed, lift, tip, violation), tiled frames from the videos at those moments for all 26 tasks, compared the two same-seed repeats task by task, and put successes next to failures of the same task to see where they first differ. It also wrote a per-step recorder for actions, joint state and jar pose, which the next GPU rental will switch on. The one simulator episode planned never ran: the pod's driver (595) crashes Isaac Sim's renderer at startup, so simulation stays on the 580-driver Vast hosts. Cost about \$1.50.

\textbf{What we found.}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
  \item The approach is the same every time. Repeats of one seed reach the jar within half a second of each other and look identical until the fingers meet the lid. Everything that decides the outcome happens after contact.
  \item Two moments decide it: whether the push gets the lid over, and the regrasp afterwards, when the gripper leaves the lid, descends, and lands on the body or on the rim. Seven of ten same-seed pairs split in that regrasp.
  \item Idling is as common as crashing: 36 episodes end with nothing lifted and no violation, half of them with the lid already closed and the robot parked. The biggest crash story is tipping the jar after closing it, 19 episodes.
  \item Only 8 tasks behave the same way every run (five always tip, two always park, one always succeeds). The other 18 are knife-edge.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Where that points.} The lever is in execution and recovery after contact, not in the approach: a slower or shallower regrasp, a stop when a finger meets the rim, a retry when the push has stalled. The leverage note ranks three stage 2 experiments, each about 200 episodes per condition. Panels are frozen: development 0, 3, 6, 9, 15, 17; held out 2, 5, 8, 14, 16, 23, 7, 18, 12.

\textbf{Where it lives.} Repository, under \texttt{progress/receipts/}: the frame sheets (\texttt{02-frames/}), the pair strips (\texttt{02-pairs/}), the timelines, sensitivity map and task notes (\texttt{02-*.md}); the same sheets are in the dataset under \texttt{analysis/02/}. The leverage note, \emph{Where the leverage is}, sits with the other research notes under \texttt{docs/research/}.

\section*{Task 3: The first knob, retry when the robot stalls}

\textbf{Why.} Task 2 said the lever is after contact, and that a third of the episodes end with the robot parked. The simplest fix anyone would try is a retry: if the arm has stopped moving, let go, go back to where it started, and let the policy try again. Retry also has the best track record of the cheap fixes in the literature. Before evolving anything we wanted to know whether this one knob moves safe completion at all, and whether a scripted retreat tips the jar.

\textbf{What we did.}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
  \item Built a small proxy between the simulator and the policy server. Off, it passes every byte through unchanged (checked). On, it watches the arm's joints and, if none has moved more than 0.01 rad in 4 seconds, replaces the policy's next four chunks with a scripted retreat: open the gripper, glide back to the first pose, hand control back. At most three times per episode. Policy and benchmark untouched.
  \item Froze the plan before the first episode: the six development tasks, seeds 100 to 115, every seed once with the proxy off and once on. 192 episodes, 96 pairs, two GPUs on one Vast host, 7 hours, about \$6.
  \item Scored safe success and violations per arm, and a paired McNemar test over the 96 pairs.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Result.} Counts over 96 episodes per arm.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrr}
\toprule
 & Safe success & Unsafe success & Safe fail & Unsafe fail & Violations \\
\midrule
Native & 17 (18\%) & 7 & 31 & 41 & 48 (50\%) \\
Retry & 16 (17\%) & 9 & 33 & 38 & 47 (49\%) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Paired: retry minus native is $-1$ pair on safe success and $-1$ pair on violations, $p = 1.0$ for both. The knob, as frozen, changes nothing.

\textbf{What we learned.}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
  \item The retreat fired in 21 of 96 retry episodes, and none of those 21 ended in success. Their native twins succeeded six times. The mechanism itself worked: the arm went home cleanly, no jar tipped during a retreat, and the policy resumed every time. It then parked again, or tipped the jar on the second attempt (5 of the 21). Resetting the arm does not reset the situation.
  \item It fired rarely because the stall test is wrong for this policy. A parked arm still jitters by 0.02 to 0.1 rad over 4 seconds, above our 0.01 threshold, so the detector only tripped when the gripper was pressed hard and motionless on the jar, the one stall a go-home cannot fix.
  \item The other 75 pairs are two runs of the identical episode, because the proxy did nothing in them. They agreed on safe success 85\% of the time and on violations 64\% of the time. The simulator's renderer is not reproducible across launches, so a same-seed pair shares its layout but never its pixels. That is the noise floor every later knob has to beat, and it rules out common random numbers for the search.
  \item Half of all episodes end in a violation, and the retreat never touched that. Stalls and tipped jars are different failures; this knob only ever addressed the first.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Where that points.} A stall needs a definition this policy can satisfy, the jar not moving rather than the arm not moving, and the bigger prize is the violation rate. The next knob aims there.

In the repository: plan, run log, scoring, identity check and frame sheets under \path{progress/receipts/03-*}; the proxy under \path{scripts/todo3/}.

\section*{Task 4: Do the native commands produce the requested motion?}

\textbf{Why.} Task 2 showed where failures happen, but could not tell us whether the arm fails to follow its commands or follows unhelpful commands. That distinction matters before investing in an execution adapter inspired by the literature.

\textbf{What we did.} Froze twelve native episodes on development tasks 0, 3, 6, 9, 15 and 17 at seeds 300 and 301. Two GPU lanes split the work on one new Vast host, with the paper's control and rendering protocol. A passive recorder saved every proposed chunk, executed target, measured joint state, contact event, monitor trace and both camera videos. All twelve completed on their first attempt and passed alignment checks. We inspected every episode's joint plots and event frames, including successes and safe failures. No intervention or held-out task was used.

\textbf{Result.} Two safe successes, two unsafe successes, four safe failures and four unsafe failures. Safe requested completion was 2/12, violations 6/12 and engagement 12/12. Eight episodes never reached the goal. These are diagnostic counts on a different host, not a new pooled benchmark rate.

\textbf{What we learned.}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
  \item Five of six violating episodes tracked closely in the two seconds ending at their first violation. Both unsafe successes carried a jar that never closed. Three safe unfinished episodes closed the lid but never grasped or lifted it. Missing continuation and poor interaction choices remain the broader explanation.
  \item One important exception, task 6 seed 301, developed a substantial discrepancy after closure and before tipping: mean post-action joint RMS error 0.0151 rad, against 0.000102 before contact. Physical joint limits do not explain that window. The trace cannot distinguish a causal tracking fault from resistance during an already bad contact.
  \item Large later errors often came from commands beyond joint limits. A separately labeled post-hoc source audit explains part, not all, of them. Increasing gain is not automatically the remedy. A successful episode also waited a long time after closure, so a slow interval alone is not a reliable failure detector.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Where that points.} No general tracking deficit is established, but the contact exception keeps a bounded adapter hypothesis open. Any adapter comparison remains exploratory and must demonstrate safe completion, not just smaller tracking error. Command and continuation steering remains the stronger broader direction. We still need a working lever before an evolutionary claim.

\textbf{Cost and record.} The two-lane queue took 42 minutes after setup: 20,121 policy controls, 120 warm-up controls and 2,520 model queries including four model-only probes. Every recording was copied locally and hash-checked. Receipts are \path{progress/receipts/04-tracking-*}; full evidence belongs under \path{recordings/tracking04}, \path{jar/tracking04_native} and \path{receipts/tracking04} in the private dataset. The preservation receipt records the pinned revision and final rental state.
